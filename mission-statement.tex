    % Q1. Academic Identity: Before writing your research vision, start by thinking about yourself as a researcher. What are you passionate about? What is the change you wish to see in the world? What do you enjoy doing? Which methods and skills are you good at

    %  I use algorithms, automation and computation to study electronic materials, such as 2D crystal materials, topological materials, and organic semiconductors, in order to demonstrate demonstrate how new materials can be utilised to build new devices and technologies.

    My research is driven by a fundamental intrigue of novel electronic materials, to demonstrate reliable processability and physical properties useful for modern electronic devices. To do so requires optimising complex synthesis and control systems for in-situ characterisation. This interest has emerged through my background spanning condensed matter physics, electronic materials-science engineering, scientific software development, where I have repeatedly encountered the same core challenge: modern experiments generate vast, complex datasets and rely on intricate instrumentation, however much work performed across the device life-cycle is still manual; sample-fabrication, controlled measurement, analysis, optimisation, cycling back to sample-fabrication. My central goal is to bridge this gap through automation and, ultimately, autonomous scientific discovery. In particular I want to demonstrate control over the creation of complex materials samples that exhibit novel electronic properties, and autonomously investigate their physical phase space.

    \pind During my PhD and postdoctoral work in electronic materials, I developed strong expertise in experimental physics across synchrotron, neutron and nanofabrication facilities. Research efforts include crystal-growth/transfer methods, scattering characterisation, and cryogenic electronic transport; all of which employ a wide range of control systems to investigate electronic systems such as layered 2D, topological and organic semiconducting materials. All these materials share a common theme: they require complex, multi-dimensional synthesis and control systems to achieve the desired material properties. For example, layered 2D crystals have strong surface sensitivity to adjacent layers, precluding physical vapour deposition methods due to induced damage, and making clean layer-transfer methods a requirement for state-of-the-art electronic-structures.
    In organic semiconducting polymers, structure-property relationships emerge from a highly complex and coupled processing space. Dispensing methods such as blade coating and spin coating, combined with post-processing steps like thermal annealing, create a high-dimensional parameter space that governs nanoscopic morphology, crystallinity, and ultimately device performance. 

    \pind Critically, these materials exemplify the challenges of modern experimental science: the relationship between processing conditions and resulting properties is non-linear and highly sensitive, history-dependent, and difficult to probe directly, thus requiring automated approaches. Some of my work utilising synchrotron X-ray scattering has focused on integrating in-situ measurements of solution processing to capture the evolution of material structure in real time, generating rich, multi-dimensional datasets that encode this complex phase space. However, extracting insight from such data and using it to guide experimental decisions remains a largely manual and intuition-driven process. This experience has reinforced my interest in automation not just as a tool for efficiency, but as a necessary step toward navigating complex experimental landscapes.

    \pind Part of my postdoctoral focus has been to developed modern software pipelines and scientific tools (e.g., waveguiding simulations for x-ray scattering characterisation, and NEXAFS and Kramers-Kronig transformation toolkits for analysis) that are robust, reusable and conform to modern software engineering standards to encourage update and reuse. While such examples are not control systems per se, they represent a step towards more trustworthy, integrated, and automated scientific workflows that can be easily adopted and extended by other researchers. Such approaches to automated control systems for modular use in experimental labs and in setup-packup facility experiments are essential for creating consistent, thorough progress. These efforts reduce manual intervention and enabling more systematic exploration of parameter spaces across varying equipment, allowing researchers to focus on higher-level insight.

    \pind Beyond automation, I am particularly motivated by the concept of autonomous discovery: systems that can iteratively explore high-dimensional experimental spaces and optimize measurement strategies in time-intensive work environments, toward identifying phases and optimal conditions with minimal human intervention. The processing landscape of polymer materials provides a compelling testbed for such ideas, where large phases spaces can be generated but are difficult to navigate in a timely fashion. Such ideas can be extended to layered 2D materials which involve growth and transfer processes, and even further to a wide range of experimental contexts across science and engineering departments.

    \pind Looking forward, I aim to contribute to research in control systems, autonomous systems and AI applied to experimental science. This includes applications such as automated laboratories, adaptive sensing platforms, and robotic systems capable of interacting with complex physical environments. More broadly, I am interested in how autonomous systems can be deployed to navigate complex phase spaces—whether in materials science, manufacturing, or other domains where high-dimensional optimisation is a central challenge. Ultimately, my research vision is to help transition scientific practice from manual, sequential workflows to integrated, autonomous systems capable of accelerating discovery. By combining automation, advanced data analysis, and intelligent control, I aim to contribute to a future where scientific systems are not just tools, but active participants in the process of discovery.
